\begin{abstract}
Medical, cold-chain and dangerous-goods distribution ask that a routing plan
overflow its vehicles with probability at most $\gamma$, with $\gamma$
between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-6}$. We study how to verify such a level for a
given plan of the vehicle routing problem with simultaneous pickup and
delivery. Under gamma demands with a Gaussian factor copula, the log
asymptotics of a route's peak load have two end modes --- total delivery at
the start, total pickup at the end --- with scales from secular equations.
This anatomy explains when single-shift importance sampling fails: on routes
whose two end scales are close, a single cross-entropy shift underestimates
the overflow probability by a median of $4$--$5\%$ (up to $40\%$ at the 90\%
quantile) while its relative variance stays near $5$, so the error is not
visible. We give a stage-mixture estimator placed at computed dominating
points, with no pilot simulation, and extend it to a route-day simulator with
stochastic travel and service times, where no exact method exists, and to
hubs whose routes share demand factors, with a calibrated capacity search.
On 148 instances built from population, postal and road data for Northern
Vietnam and Guangdong, the route estimator agrees with an exact conditional
dynamic program within $0.82\%$ in every case where the program applies,
and the route-day estimator agrees with $10^7$-draw Monte Carlo within its
error. \pending{one sentence on cost against the exact conditional program
and on hub buffers.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Planning models for stochastic vehicle routing control risk through chance
constraints, robust counterparts or distributionally robust ambiguity sets
\citep{GounarisWiesemannFloudas2013,DinhFukasawaLuedtke2018,GhosalWiesemann2020};
see \citet{GendreauJabaliRei2016} for a review. They evaluate route risk inside
the optimiser at moderate levels, with exact formulas, approximations or
sampling. Applications with strict service levels need a step after
optimisation: an estimate, with a confidence interval, showing that the plan
actually produced meets $\P(\text{overflow}) \le \gamma$ for
$\gamma \in [10^{-6}, 10^{-3}]$, for every route and for the plan as a whole,
at the scale of a regional network.

At such levels crude Monte Carlo needs $1.96^2/(\varepsilon^2\gamma)$ draws for
relative error $\varepsilon$ at 95\% confidence --- $3.8\times10^{8}$ draws per
route at $\gamma=10^{-6}$ and $\varepsilon=0.1$. Importance sampling (IS) is the
standard remedy, and mean shifts tuned by the cross-entropy (CE) method are
the practitioner's default \citep{deBoerEtAl2005}. The failure of a
pickup-and-delivery route is a union of events --- the vehicle can overflow
after any stop --- and single-mode IS can then fail without warning
\citep{GlassermanWang1997}. Mixtures over failure modes
\citep{OwenMaximovChertkov2019,KurtzSong2013,GeyerPapaioannouStraub2019} cure
this when the modes are known, at the price of pilot simulation when they are
not.

\emph{Where IS is needed.} For the demand-only route model used in planning
there is an exact alternative: given the copula factors the demands are
independent, so the overflow probability is a two-dimensional integral of a
one-dimensional dynamic program. We implement it and use it both as the
reference and as a competitor (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). It is accurate
and, on the short routes of our plans, \pending{fast or slow, from the
baseline run}. It does not extend to route days with stochastic travel and
service times and time windows, where the event couples loads and times;
there the mixture estimator is, to our knowledge, the only efficient method,
and the demand-only model is the setting in which its construction can be
checked against an exact answer.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) We formulate the certification of a given plan at service levels
$10^{-3}$--$10^{-6}$, route-wise and hub-wise, as a post-optimisation step.
(ii) We derive the logarithmic asymptotics of the peak load of a
pickup-and-delivery route under gamma marginals and a one-factor Gaussian
copula: two end modes with spectral scales (Theorem~\ref{thm:ld}, proof
sketch).
(iii) We show when single-shift IS loses logarithmic efficiency
(Theorem~\ref{thm:single}) and measure its finite-level bias on real routes:
the asymptotic threshold ($\varrho>1/4$) holds for every route of our plans,
but material bias appears only for $\varrho>0.75$, and it is invisible in the
reported standard error at $\gamma\le10^{-5}$.
(iv) We give a pilot-free stage-mixture estimator (SMIS) at computed
dominating points, its extension to a route-day simulator without exact
solution (MDIS), and a hub estimator for routes with shared factors, with
attribution of hub risk to routes and a calibrated capacity search.
(v) We evaluate on 148 region-scale instances against an exact conditional
program, Glasserman--Li two-step IS, conditional Monte Carlo, subset
simulation and CE.
The IS ingredients are known --- dominating points
\citep{SadowskyBucklew1990}, multiple-path failure of single shifts
\citep{GlassermanWang1997}, mixtures as subsolutions \citep{DupuisWang2007},
factor-copula two-step schemes \citep{GlassermanLi2005,BassambooJunejaZeevi2008}
--- and we do not claim new IS theory. What is new is their form for the
pickup-and-delivery peak, the measured failure regime, and a certification
procedure for regional plans.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\emph{Risk in vehicle routing.} Chance-constrained and robust models
\citep{GounarisWiesemannFloudas2013,DinhFukasawaLuedtke2018,GhosalWiesemann2020}
handle route risk inside the optimisation with exact formulas, moment bounds or
scenario sampling \citep{GendreauJabaliRei2016}. Hazardous-materials routing
measures risk as accident probability times consequence along links
\citep{ErkutVerter1998}. We found no work that certifies an already optimised
routing plan at rare levels with IS, and none that treats the mid-route peak
of the simultaneous pickup-and-delivery load \citep{Dethloff2001} as a rare
event. The closest IS precedent in logistics is stockout simulation in
production--inventory systems \citep{GlassermanLiu1996}.

\emph{Rare-event simulation.} Efficiency notions and dominating points are
surveyed by \citet{AsmussenGlynn2007}; \citet{GlassermanWang1997} show that a
single shift is inefficient when the rare set has several ways of occurring;
\citet{DupuisWang2007} build efficient schemes from subsolutions, of which
mixtures are instances; \citet{OwenMaximovChertkov2019} bound the variance of a
mixture that samples each event of a union conditionally; CE with Gaussian
mixtures handles multimodal failure regions
\citep{KurtzSong2013,GeyerPapaioannouStraub2019,PapaioannouGeyerStraub2019}.
For Gaussian factor copulas, \citet{GlassermanLi2005} combine a factor shift
with conditional exponential twisting, and \citet{BassambooJunejaZeevi2008}
treat extremal dependence; for quadratic losses of Gaussian inputs,
\citet{GlassermanHeidelbergerShahabuddin2000} use the same eigenstructure that
gives our scales. Subset simulation \citep{AuBeck2001} and sequential Monte
Carlo splitting \citep{CerouDelMoralFuronGuyader2012} need no mode
information and are our model-free rivals.

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

A route visits customers $1,\dots,L$. The vehicle leaves the depot with all
deliveries $d_1,\dots,d_L$ and collects pickups $p_1,\dots,p_L$. The load after
stop $k$ is $L_k = D_k + P_k$ with $D_k = \sum_{i>k} d_i$ and
$P_k = \sum_{i\le k} p_i$ ($k=0,\dots,L$), the peak is $F = \max_k L_k$, and
the route overflows a capacity $c$ if $F > c$.

\emph{Demand model.} Each $d_i$ is gamma with coefficient of variation cv,
shape $\kappa = 1/\mathrm{cv}^2$ and scale $\theta^d_i = \bar d_i/\kappa$,
likewise $p_i$. Dependence is a one-factor Gaussian copula per demand type:
$d_i = G_{d,i}^{-1}(\Phi(Z_{d,i}))$ with
$Z_{d,i} = \sqrt{\rho}\,W_d + \sqrt{1-\rho}\,E_{d,i}$, and similarly for
pickups with a factor $W_p$ independent of $W_d$. The input
$u = (W_d, W_p, E_d, E_p)$ is standard normal. All customers of a hub share the
two factors; hubs have separate factors. The baseline is $\mathrm{cv}=0.3$ and
$\rho=0.6$; Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} varies both, the pickup-to-delivery
ratio and the marginal family. The independence of $W_d$ and $W_p$ is
load-bearing: it makes the two end blocks independent, which the theory below
uses.

\begin{definition}[Certificate]
Given $\gamma$, a relative precision $\varepsilon$ and a confidence $1-\delta$
(we use $\delta=0.05$), a certificate for a route or a hub is an unbiased
estimate $\hat p$ of its overflow probability $p$ whose central-limit
confidence interval has half-width at most $\varepsilon\hat p$, together with
the check $\hat p(1+\varepsilon) \le \gamma$; for a hub, also the attribution of
$p$ to its routes. The interval is asymptotic; Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}
reports its empirical coverage.
\end{definition}

\section{Structure of the overflow event}
\label{sec:structure}

For an index set $S$ with gamma scales $\theta_S$ let
$C_S = \rho\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top + (1-\rho)I$ and
\[
\lambda(S) = \lambda_{\max}\!\left(\Theta_S^{1/2} C_S \Theta_S^{1/2}\right),
\]
the largest root of $1 = \rho\sum_{i\in S}\theta_i/(\lambda-(1-\rho)\theta_i)$.
Write $\lambda^d_k = \lambda(\{i>k\})$ for the delivery block of stage $k$,
$\lambda^p_k = \lambda(\{i\le k\})$ for its pickup block,
$\lambda_k = \max(\lambda^d_k,\lambda^p_k)$,
$\lambda^\star = \max(\lambda^d_0,\lambda^p_L)$ and
$\varrho = \min(\lambda^d_0,\lambda^p_L)/\lambda^\star \in (0,1]$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sandwich}
$\max(D_0, P_L) \le F \le D_0 + P_L$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[proof sketch]\label{thm:ld}
As $c\to\infty$ with the route fixed and unbounded gamma marginals,
$\log\P(L_k > c) \sim -c/\lambda_k$ and $\log\P(F > c) \sim -c/\lambda^\star$.
\end{theorem}

The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) is a sketch: the uniformity of the
logarithmic remainder of the gamma quantile (Lemma~A1) is not written out. Our
simulator clips $\Phi$ at $1-10^{-15}$, so its demands are bounded and the
theorem describes the unclipped model; at our thresholds the clipping is
inactive except where reported.

So, on the logarithmic scale, a route overflows at the start (delivery block)
or at the end (pickup block). Middle stages have strictly smaller scales: the
stage where the two partial blocks cross has $\lambda_k\le\lambda^\star/2$ on
homogeneous routes. A stage $k$ carries relative mass
$\exp(-c(1/\lambda_k-1/\lambda^\star)(1+o(1)))$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mass}); with $c\approx\lambda^\star\log(1/\gamma)$ the
weaker end mode carries $\gamma^{(1/\varrho-1)(1+o(1))}$. This is qualitative
guidance only:

\begin{remark}[Pre-asymptotic regime]
For a three-stop test route with $\mathrm{cv}=0.3$, the exact dominating-point
energy is $16\%$ of its asymptotic value $c/\lambda^\star$ at
$P\approx 9\times10^{-4}$ and $35\%$ at $P\approx 1.6\times10^{-9}$; the
logarithmic corrections of the gamma quantile are large at practical levels.
The estimators therefore use computed dominating points, and the finite-level
mass of a mode is measured by $\exp(-(E_m-E_{\min}))$ with exact energies $E_m$
(Section~\ref{sec:single}).
\end{remark}

for one Northern Vietnam and one Guangdong route.

\section{When a single shift fails}
\label{sec:single}

Mean-shift IS with shift $\tau$ uses the proposal $N(\tau,I)$ and weight
$w(u) = \exp(-\langle u,\tau\rangle + |\tau|^2/2)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:single}
Let $\tau^\star$ be the asymptotic dominating point of the dominant end mode.
(a) If $\varrho > 1/4$, the shift $\tau^\star$ is not logarithmically efficient:
$\log m_2(\tau^\star) - 2\log\P(F>c) \ge c\,(4/\lambda^\star - 1/(\varrho\lambda^\star))(1+o(1))>0$.
(b) (proof sketch) If $\varrho \le 1/4$ and the dominant end block has second
eigenvalue at most $\lambda^\star/4$, the shift is logarithmically efficient.
\end{theorem}

Part (a) rests on Theorem~\ref{thm:ld}; (a) and (b) have different hypotheses,
so $1/4$ is not a sharp threshold. The extra second moment in (a) comes from
a region the proposal almost never visits, so the sample variance does not
reveal it, and at finite levels the estimate is biased low by about the mass
of the missed mode.

\emph{What the data show.} The asymptotic criterion is uninformative for our
plans: all 5\,920 routes with two or more customers have $\varrho>1/4$
(Guangdong median $0.36$, Northern Vietnam median $0.93$). The practical
regime is narrower. On 120 routes (Table~\ref{tab:routes}) the single CE shift
has median error $0.10$--$0.27\%$ when $\varrho\le0.75$, close to the mixture
estimators, and $2.9$, $5.1$, $6.0$ and $6.1\%$ (90\% quantile $7.6$, $21.7$,
$26.7$, $39.9\%$) when $\varrho>0.75$ at $\gamma=10^{-3},\dots,10^{-6}$. The
error is an underestimate in $62\%$ of these rows at $10^{-3}$ and in
$93$--$95\%$ at $\gamma\le10^{-4}$ (median signed error $-4.3$ to $-4.7\%$). It
is visible at $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$, where the single shift's relative
variance is $512$ and $87$, and invisible at $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-6}$, where it
is $5.2$ and $5.5$ (reported relative standard error $0.2\%$ at $10^6$ draws,
against a $6\%$ bias). Figure~\ref{fig:bias} plots the measured bias against
$\varrho$ and against the computed mass of the missed mode.

Two cautions. First, in our instances $\varrho>0.75$ occurs only in Northern
Vietnam, where the pickup rate was set equal to the delivery rate because no
regional pickup statistic is published; region, $\varrho$ and that assumption
are confounded. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} breaks the confounding by
scaling the pickup means of the same routes. Second, $\varrho$ is computed in
closed form from the route data, so the diagnostic is free, but the threshold
$0.75$ is empirical.

\section{Estimators}
\label{sec:estimators}

\paragraph{SMIS.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:smis} computes, for every stage $k$ and
block, a minimum-norm point $u_m$ of $\{L_k(u)\ge c\}$ by constrained local
minimisation (SLSQP) started on the block's Perron direction, with the exact
quantile transform. The set is not convex, so $u_m$ is a computed local
minimiser, not a certified global one. Modes with energy
$E_m=|u_m|^2/2$ within $\log(1/\eta)$ of the smallest are kept, and the
estimator samples from
\[
q = a_0\,N(0,I) + \sum_m a_m\,N(u_m, I),\qquad a_m\propto e^{-(E_m-E_{\min})},
\]
with likelihood ratio
$\varphi(u)/q(u) = \big(a_0 + \sum_m a_m e^{\langle u,u_m\rangle-|u_m|^2/2}\big)^{-1}$.
The tuning constants are $\eta=10^{-3}$ (hubs $10^{-4}$) and $a_0=0.05$, fixed
for every experiment; there is no pilot simulation. An optional variant
(SMIS3) adds $3$ points on arcs between modes of adjacent stages; it changes
nothing measurable on our routes and we report $n_{\rm arc}=0$ (SMIS0)
throughout. Theorem~\ref{thm:mixture} (Appendix) is a sketch of logarithmic
efficiency under spectral-gap conditions; at $\rho=0.6$ they hold for every end
block of our plans, at $\rho=0.3$ for $0.4\%$ of them, and
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} tests SMIS in both cases.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{SMIS for one route}\label{alg:smis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require route means $\bar d,\bar p$; cv, $\rho$; capacity $c$; $\eta$, $a_0$, $N$
\For{$k=0,\dots,L$ and block $b\in\{d,p\}$}
  \State $v\gets$ Perron vector of $\Theta_b^{1/2}C\Theta_b^{1/2}$ on the block's inputs
  \State $u_{k,b}\gets\arg\min\{|u|^2/2 : L_k(u)\ge c\}$ by SLSQP from the ray along $v$
  \Comment{skip if $c$ is unreachable}
\EndFor
\State keep modes with $E_m\le E_{\min}+\log(1/\eta)$; $a_m\gets(1-a_0)e^{-(E_m-E_{\min})}/\sum_{m'}e^{-(E_{m'}-E_{\min})}$
\State draw $u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(N)}\sim q$; $\hat p\gets N^{-1}\sum_j \mathbf 1\{F(u^{(j)})>c\}\,\varphi(u^{(j)})/q(u^{(j)})$
\State \Return $\hat p$, its standard error, ESS
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Cost: $2(L+1)$ local minimisations of dimension $2+2L$, each a few dozen
quantile evaluations, then $N$ simulator calls and $N\times(\#\text{modes})$
inner products.

\paragraph{MDIS.} The same construction applies to any simulator that exposes
its failure modes as scalar functions $g_m(u) > 0$ of a Gaussian input. We
use it for a route-day model (Section~\ref{sec:routeday}): demands as above,
lognormal travel times per leg $T_l=\bar t_l\exp(\sigma_T(\sqrt{\rho_T}V+
\sqrt{1-\rho_T}H_l)-\sigma_T^2/2)$ with $\bar t_l$ the road distance at
$40$\,km/h, $\sigma_T=0.25$ and a shared congestion factor ($\rho_T=0.5$),
service times of $10$ minutes plus $6$ minutes per tonne handled, and a failure
if the vehicle overflows at any stage, arrives at any stop later than its
nominal time plus a slack $s$, or returns later than nominal plus $2s$.
Dominating points of the load, lateness and return modes are computed from
several structured starts. No exact method applies: the event couples a
maximum of copula sums with lognormal sums and load-dependent service.

\paragraph{Hubs.} The routes of a hub share the factors, so the hub event
$\bigcup_r\{F_r > c\}$ is again a union of stage events, now in dimension
$2+2n$ for $n$ customers. Every route's SMIS modes are embedded in the hub
input (the two factors and that route's coordinates, zeros elsewhere), modes
are kept over all routes with $\eta=10^{-4}$, and the likelihood ratio
\[
\log\frac{\varphi(x)}{q(x)} = -\log\Big(a_0+\sum_m a_m\exp\big(\langle x,\theta_m\rangle-|\theta_m|^2/2\big)\Big)
\]
is computed with sparse inner products, since $\theta_m$ has $2+2L_r$ nonzero
coordinates. From the same draws we estimate each route's overflow
probability and the probability that it is the only route to overflow --- an
attribution of hub risk to routes. A route that cannot reach $c$ contributes
no mode and zero probability.

\paragraph{Capacity calibration.} To report the buffer a hub needs for
$\gamma$, we search for $c$ with $\hat P_{\rm hub}(c)=\gamma$: a Laplace-type
proxy $\log\sum_r e^{-E_r(c)}=\log\gamma$ gives a starting point $c_0$ (the
proxy is not a bound and was $15$--$45$ times off in a first run), then at most
three secant steps on $\log\hat P_{\rm hub}(c)-\log\gamma$ with $2\times10^4$
hub draws per evaluation, stopping when $|\log(\hat P_{\rm hub}/\gamma)|<\log1.25$;
the final $\hat P_{\rm hub}$ uses $10^5$ draws. The buffer is $b=c/Q-1$.

\paragraph{Exact conditional reference.} Given $(W_d,W_p)$ the demands are
independent, so $\P(F>c\mid W)$ is a one-dimensional dynamic program over the
discretised load (we use $V=256$ and $512$ levels with Richardson
extrapolation), and
$\P(F>c)=\iint\P(F>c\mid w)\,\varphi(w_d)\varphi(w_p)\,dw$ is a product
trapezoid on $[-6,9]^2$. For a hub, the routes are conditionally independent,
so $\P_{\rm hub}=\iint[1-\prod_r(1-\P(F_r>c\mid w))]\varphi\varphi\,dw$. The
error estimate combines the Richardson difference and a nested-grid
quadrature difference.

\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Instances and plans}
We built 148 instances, one per depot area: the 25 provinces of Northern
Vietnam (Hanoi split into two hubs) and 122 county-level areas of Guangdong
(Table~\ref{tab:instances}). Service points aggregate Kontur population
hexagons (15\,000 people per point in Vietnam, 4\,000 in Guangdong) by
weighted $k$-means; distances are OSRM road distances. Daily demand rates
come from official postal statistics: $0.087$ parcels per person per day in
Vietnam (3.2 billion items in 2024) and, for Guangdong, $0.34$ received and
$0.91$ sent per person per day (2024 volumes). The following are assumptions:
1~kg per parcel; truck capacities of 15 (Vietnam) and 30 (Guangdong) tonnes;
the Vietnamese pickup rate equal to the delivery rate (no regional figure is
published). The service points represent 115.5 million people in the
Kontur grid (official 2023 figures: 127.8 million); provinces follow the
pre-2025 boundaries. Plans are solved by ALNS with the sample-average chance
gate; we certify these plans. Routes are short: the median route has 6
(Guangdong) and 7 (Vietnam) stops.

\subsection{Routes under the demand model}
\label{sec:routes}
We use 120 routes (80 Guangdong, 40 Northern Vietnam; 4 to 15 stops, median
7) and four levels $\gamma\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5},10^{-6}\}$; $c$ is set so
that the overflow probability is close to $\gamma$. Every estimator uses
$N=10^6$ draws. The CE mixture uses a pilot of $0.8$--$1.2\times10^5$ draws per
route. Table~\ref{tab:routes} reports errors against the reference.

\emph{Against the exact program.} On the 89 routes with $L\le8$ at
$\gamma\in\{10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$ (178 cases), SMIS deviates from the exact
conditional program by $0.15$ and $0.16\%$ in median, $0.38$ and $0.44\%$ at the
90\% quantile, and at most $0.82\%$; the deviation is within $1.96$ reported
standard errors in $90\%$ and $87\%$ of cases (the program has its own
discretisation error). \pending{exact-program comparison at $10^{-6}$ and for
$L>8$ from the baseline run.}

\emph{Across all levels.} SMIS has relative error at most $0.61\%$ at the
90\% quantile in every $\varrho$ band with more than one route, relative
variance $3.8$--$5.9$ (CE mixture $3.8$--$6.0$, single shift $3.5$--$5.5$
where it is unbiased), $2$--$7$ components, and a median build time of
$0.39$--$0.86$\,s. At $\gamma\le10^{-5}$ the reference for routes without an
exact program is the CE mixture.

\emph{Samples.} Counting CE's pilot, the CE mixture needs $224$--$276$ times
more draws than SMIS for $\varepsilon=0.1$ at 95\% confidence and $3.2$--$3.8$
times more for $\varepsilon=0.01$: the advantage is the saved pilot, not a
smaller variance.

\subsection{Route days without an exact method}
\label{sec:routeday}
On 60 routes we set the slack $s$ and the capacity so that the load modes and
the time modes each carry about half of the failure probability --- a design
that makes the failure multimodal on purpose, to test the estimator where a
single shift is known to struggle. The threshold search is common to all
methods and not counted. MDIS keeps $6$--$8$ modes (median) of the $20$--$30$
candidates; $16$--$21$ candidate modes per route cannot reach their threshold
and are dropped. Table~\ref{tab:routeday} gives the results. At
$\gamma=10^{-3}$, against $10^7$-draw crude Monte Carlo (relative standard
error about $1\%$), MDIS deviates by $0.76\%$ in median and $1.77\%$ at the 90\%
quantile, CE by $0.89\%$ and $1.75\%$, and the single CE shift by $2.2\%$ and
$22\%$. At $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$ there is no crude reference; MDIS and the CE
mixture, built independently from the same simulator, agree within $0.24\%$ in
median and $0.59\%$ at the 90\% quantile. MDIS is built in $3$--$4$\,s against
$43$--$55$\,s for the CE pilot, and CE needs about $1\,800$ times more draws for
$\varepsilon=0.1$ and $18$--$19$ times more for $\varepsilon=0.01$.

\subsection{A note on quantum amplitude estimation}
Quantum amplitude estimation reduces the number of simulator calls for a
probability quadratically \citep{Brassard2002,Montanaro2015}. On the same 120
routes, counting oracle calls only (no qubit or error-correction cost) with
explicit constants for a rigorous schedule, it needs $0.003$--$0.5$ times the
calls of crude Monte Carlo, but $27$--$3\,550$ times more than the sampling
stage of classical IS ($2.5$--$380$ times more if the IS pilot is counted).
With an IS-equipped oracle it needs fewer calls than classical IS only in an
idealised accounting at $\varepsilon\le3\%$ ($0.14$--$0.48$ times). On this
problem the mode structure, not the quadratic speed-up, is what makes
certification cheap.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Rare overflow of a pickup-and-delivery route has a simple anatomy on the
logarithmic scale --- two end modes with closed-form scales --- that tells
when a single IS shift is at risk. In our plans the asymptotic criterion
holds everywhere, while a material and silent bias appears only on routes
with nearly balanced end scales. A mixture at computed dominating points
removes it without pilot simulation, extends to route days without an exact
method, and to hubs with shared factors. \pending{conclusion on cost against
the exact program and on hub buffers.} Open points: full proofs at the stated
generality, region-wide factors that couple hubs, correlated pickup and
delivery factors, and demand and travel models estimated from operator data.

\appendix
\section{Proofs and sketches}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:sandwich}.} $L_0 = D_0$ and $L_L = P_L$
are stage loads, and $D_k\le D_0$, $P_k\le P_L$ for all $k$. \qed

\paragraph{Lemma A1 (quantile; stated without proof).} For the gamma law,
$x(z) = G^{-1}(\Phi(z)) = \theta z^2/2 + \theta(2\kappa-1)\log z + O(1)$ as
$z\to\infty$, and $0\le x(z)\le x(0)$ for $z\le 0$.

\paragraph{Proof sketch of Theorem~\ref{thm:ld}.} Upper bound: by Lemma A1,
$D_k\le \tfrac12 Z^\top\Theta Z + R$ with $R$ a sum of logarithmic terms; for
$t<1/\lambda$, $\E\exp(t\,\tfrac12 Z^\top\Theta Z) =
\det(I - t\Theta^{1/2}C\Theta^{1/2})^{-1/2}<\infty$, so Chernoff's bound gives the
rate $1/\lambda$, and $R$ is negligible if Lemma A1 holds uniformly (not
written out). The delivery and pickup blocks have disjoint inputs, so their
moment generating functions multiply and $L_k$ has rate $1/\lambda_k$. Lower
bound: a ball of radius $O(1)$ around $(1+\eta)\sqrt{2c/\lambda}\,u^\star$, with
$u^\star = B^\top\Theta^{1/2}v/\sqrt\lambda$ ($Z=Bu$, $BB^\top = C$, $v$ the Perron
vector), lies in the event. For $F$ use Proposition~\ref{prop:sandwich}.

\begin{proposition}[from Theorem~\ref{thm:ld}]\label{prop:mass}
For a fixed gap $\lambda_k<\lambda^\star$,
$\P(L_k>c)/\P(F>c) = \exp(-c(1/\lambda_k-1/\lambda^\star)(1+o(1)))$.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Lemma A2.} Let $h(u) = \tfrac12 u^\top M u$ with $M=A^\top\Theta A$,
$A\ge 0$, eigenvalues $\lambda_1>\lambda_2$, $\kappa=\lambda_1/\lambda_2\ge 4$,
$h_+(u) = \tfrac12|\Theta^{1/2}(Au)_+|^2$, and $\tau = r v_1$ with
$r^2 = 2c/\lambda_1$. Then $h_+(u)\ge c$ implies $|u+\tau|^2\ge 4|\tau|^2$.
\emph{Proof.} Write $u = a v_1 + u_\perp$, $b=|u_\perp|$. If $a\ge0$, then
$c\le\tfrac12(\lambda_1a^2+\lambda_2b^2)$, so $b^2\ge\kappa(r^2-a^2)$ and
$|u+\tau|^2\ge(1-\kappa)a^2+2ar+(1+\kappa)r^2$, a concave function of $a$
whose minimum on $[0,r]$ is $\min((1+\kappa)r^2,4r^2) = 4r^2$ for $\kappa\ge3$;
for $a\ge r$, $|u+\tau|^2\ge(a+r)^2\ge4r^2$. If $a<0$, then $Av_1\ge0$ gives
$(Au)_+\le(Au_\perp)_+$, so $b^2\ge\kappa r^2\ge4r^2$. \qed

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:single}(a), given Theorem~\ref{thm:ld}.}
$\tau^\star$ acts on the dominant block's inputs only and the other end event
depends on the other block's inputs only, so $m_2\ge\E[w]\,\P(\text{other end})=
e^{|\tau^\star|^2}\P(\text{other end}) = \exp(2c/\lambda^\star - c/\lambda_{\rm other}+o(c))$,
while $2\log\P(F>c) = -2c/\lambda^\star(1+o(1))$. \qed

\paragraph{Sketch of Theorem~\ref{thm:single}(b).} Proposition~\ref{prop:sandwich}
and Lemma A2 applied to the block-diagonal form of the two end blocks; the
logarithmic remainders would be handled by Cauchy--Schwarz.

\begin{theorem}[sketch]\label{thm:mixture}
With a constant defensive weight, SMIS with modes at the asymptotic
dominating points is logarithmically efficient if every retained block has
$\kappa\ge4$ and in every retained stage one block dominates the other by a
factor of at least 4; stages with $\lambda_k\le\lambda^\star/2$ need not be
retained.
\end{theorem}

\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries Response to the reviewers}\\[0.3em]
Manuscript: \emph{Minimum Weighted Hazard-Exposure Dispatch: Complexity,
an Exact Algorithm, and an FPTAS}\\
Journal of Combinatorial Optimization --- major revision
\end{center}

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

Thank you for the careful reading and the constructive reports. We
revised the manuscript substantially. Reviewer~1's central objection,
that the theoretical results follow from the classical theory of
$1\|\sum w_jU_j$, is correct, and we have rewritten the abstract,
introduction, related work and summary table to say so plainly, to cite
the classical sources for each result, and to separate what is classical
from what we believe is new. Reviewer~1's question about the FPTAS
exposed a real weakness in our experiments (the FPTAS's rounding was
inactive in most of them), which we explain and fix. Reviewer~2's
requests for a clearer hardness proof, a self-contained proof of
Theorem~5, a shorter positioning section and a discussion of parameter
uncertainty are all addressed.

While revising we also found and corrected several errors of our own,
which we list in Section~\ref{sec:own}. Theorem numbers~2--5 and
Proposition~6 are unchanged; new results are Proposition~7,
Proposition~8 and Theorem~9, and a new ``Fact~1'' used in the proof of
Theorem~2. All numerical tables were regenerated.

\section*{Summary of the main changes}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Positioning and novelty} (abstract, Sections~1, 2, 4,
Table~2, conclusion): the results that are classical are labelled as
such, with the classical references added (Sahni 1976; Gens and Levner
1981; the Lawler--Moore dynamic program is identified with Theorem~3).
\item \textbf{New or sharpened results} (Section~4): a classification of
MWHED restrictions by which of $p,w,d$ are constant (Theorem~9);
Theorem~5 now covers $m$ identical vehicles and has a complete,
self-contained proof; unbounded worst-case ratio of deadline-order
dispatch with and without skipping (Proposition~6) and of the weighted
greedy repair (Proposition~7); tightness of the FPTAS analysis up to a
factor $1-\epsilon^2$ (Proposition~8).
\item \textbf{Hardness proof} (Theorem~2): rewritten as a direct
reduction from \textsc{Partition} with an explicit lemma (Fact~1) on
feasibility under a common deadline.
\item \textbf{Experiments} (Section~5): redesigned, with
exhaustive-search verification, instance sizes to $n=400$, a stronger
baseline, FPTAS experiments in which rounding is active, and an
adversarial family.
\item \textbf{Case study} (Section~5.6): deadline semantics clarified
(Remark~1), results recomputed, sensitivity analysis added.
\item \textbf{Discussion} (Section~6): the multi-vehicle subsection is
corrected, and parameter uncertainty is treated in a new subsection with
the two suggested references.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reviewer 1}

\begin{comment}
While the manuscript is technically correct and generally well
presented, the level of novelty of the theoretical contributions is not
sufficiently convincing. [\dots] most of the main theoretical results
appear to follow from well-known results in the scheduling literature.
[\dots] Consequently, the manuscript does not clearly establish new
complexity results, new algorithmic techniques, or substantially new
structural properties beyond those already known for the equivalent
scheduling formulation.
\end{comment}

\reply We agree. The manuscript proves that MWHED is
$1\|\sum w_jU_j$ and the general results follow from it. We have not
tried to argue otherwise. Concretely:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item The abstract and the introduction (Section~1) now say that the
basic complexity picture is inherited from scheduling theory and that we
claim no new technique for the general problem. The contributions are
split into two lists: a self-contained development of the classical
results in transportation language (Theorems~2--4), and what we believe
the framing adds (Theorems~5 and~9, Propositions~6--8).
\item Section~2 now states, for each of Theorems~2--5, which classical
result it is: Theorem~3 is the Lawler--Moore dynamic program, Theorem~4
is the classical value-scaling scheme for job sequencing with deadlines
(we added \citealt{sahni1976} and \citealt{gensl1981}, which we had
missed), Theorem~5 for one vehicle is classical unit-time job sequencing
with profits, and the hardness argument is the knapsack equivalence.
The sentence in the earlier Section~2 suggesting that the FPTAS
adaptation was ``not entirely mechanical'' has been removed, since that
overstated the point.
\item Table~2 (the summary table) has a new column giving the origin of each result
(classical / Lawler--Moore / Moore / new).
\item We removed the claim in the abstract, introduction and
conclusion that the dynamic program and the FPTAS are ``necessary'' for
any guarantee. Proposition~6 shows only that deadline-order dispatch has
no guarantee; it does not show that other polynomial algorithms with
guarantees cannot exist, and the text now says so.
\end{itemize}

\begin{comment}
1. Could the authors identify a new structural property?
\end{comment}

\reply We identify the following, with the caveat that each is
elementary once the equivalence is in place; we present them as modest
additions, not as deep results.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Theorem~9 (classification by homogeneity).} For every
restriction obtained by making some of the dispatch times $p$, weights
$w$ and deadlines $d$ constant, MWHED is polynomial if $p$ or $w$ is
constant and weakly NP-hard otherwise (already when only $d$ is
constant). In particular heterogeneous hazard deadlines never cause
hardness. This turns the informal question of which network structure
makes dispatch hard into a precise statement, and it is consistent with
the parameterized results of \citet{heegerhermelin2024}, which we now
discuss accurately (see Section~\ref{sec:own}).
\item \textbf{Theorem~5 for $m$ identical vehicles.} The equal-cost
matroid greedy extends to any number of identical vehicles, with a
complete proof (slots at each position, a counting criterion for
feasibility, the augmentation property, optimality of the greedy, and a
union--find implementation whose correctness we prove). The submitted
discussion suggested that the equal-cost case would become hard with
several vehicles; that inference was wrong (see Section~\ref{sec:own}).
Release dates, which the cited hardness result of
\citet{heegermolter2025} needs, are what break tractability.
\item \textbf{Propositions~6--8.} Deadline-order dispatch with and
without skipping, and the weighted greedy repair, have unbounded
worst-case ratio, and the standard FPTAS analysis is tight up to a factor
$1-\epsilon^2$ (the adversarial family also explains empirically why the
FPTAS is nearly exact on random instances, see below).
\end{itemize}

\begin{comment}
2. The manuscript repeatedly states the dynamic program and FPTAS as
major contributions. However, the relationship between these algorithms
and classical Lawler-Moore-type methods is not discussed in sufficient
detail. Are the algorithmic contributions truly different from known
scheduling algorithms?
\end{comment}

\reply No. Theorem~3's dynamic program is the Lawler--Moore dynamic
program (jobs in due-date order, state equal to the accepted processing
time, one test per accepted job); Remark~2 (after Theorem~3) says so and
describes exactly what our proof adds (a derivation from the
earliest-deadline-first lemma in the notation of the dispatch problem).
Theorem~4 is the classical value-scaling scheme, which Sahni (1976) and
Gens and Levner (1981) developed for job sequencing with deadlines;
Section~2 (``Scheduling with deadlines'' and ``Approximation'') now
states this, notes that faster schemes are known, and explains why we
keep the classical construction (its analysis is the cleanest, and
Proposition~8 shows it is tight). We no longer present either algorithm
as a major contribution; they are presented as a self-contained
treatment for a transportation audience.

\begin{comment}
3. Why does FPTAS = 1000 always appears in every table? Could the
authors strengthen the empirical evaluation?
\end{comment}

\reply This was a genuine weakness. The reason is that, with weights at
most $100$, the scaling factor $K=\max(1,\epsilon w_{\max}/n)$ equals
$1$ in almost all of our runs, so no rounding took place and the
``FPTAS'' coincided with the exact value-indexed dynamic program. A
ratio of $1.000$ was therefore guaranteed and said nothing about the
approximation scheme. We now state this explicitly (Section~5.2, last row of
Table~4: the share of instances with $K=1$) and have redesigned the
study (Section~5; every table was regenerated):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Verification} (Section~5.1): every algorithm is checked
against exhaustive search, including all $n!$ dispatch orders for
$n\le7$ (400 instances), best-feasible-subset enumeration for $n\le14$
(1,500 instances, one third with weights up to $10^6$, with the FPTAS
guarantee checked in $4{,}500$ runs of which $2{,}923$ had active
scaling), and exhaustive search over vehicle assignments for the
equal-cost algorithm with $m=2,3$. There were no violations.
\item \textbf{Accuracy across sizes} (Section~5.2): $n$ up to $400$, $50$
instances per size (previously $\le100$ and $20$), worst-case ratios
reported as well as means, and a stronger baseline, EDD with skipping,
in addition to naive EDD.
\item \textbf{FPTAS with active scaling} (Section~5.3, Tables~5--6,
Figure~3): weights up to $10^6$ (so $K$ is in the hundreds to
thousands), a strongly correlated family, and the adversarial family of
Proposition~8. Findings: on random and strongly correlated instances the
FPTAS is nearly exact (worst ratio $0.9993$ even at $\epsilon=0.5$)
because the bound $\epsilon w_{\max}$ is pessimistic when the optimum
contains many sites; on the adversarial family the ratio falls to
$0.669$ at $\epsilon=0.5$ and follows the guarantee as $\epsilon$
shrinks, so the guarantee is not vacuous. A simple completion step
(re-insert rejected sites greedily) repairs the family completely.
\item \textbf{Runtime} (Section~5.4): re-run with active scaling.
\end{itemize}

We chose not to report this as ``FPTAS $=1$'' evidence at all, and
the text says explicitly that the earlier tables should not be read as
evidence about the scheme.

\section{Reviewer 2}

\begin{comment}
3. The part of MWHED's positioning in page 6 is comprehensible,
nevertheless it could be rephrased better by shorter arguments.
\end{comment}

\reply The ``Positioning'' paragraph (Section~2) has been cut by roughly half; it now lists the four literatures it draws on
and refers to Table~2 for which results are classical.

\begin{comment}
1. Theorem 2 on NP-hardness of MWHED might require a bit efforts to get
full clarity. In the 3rd line from the bottom of page 9, don't you think
that the fact ``completion times along a fixed set are additive'' is too
strong? What fixed sets should we use here? [\dots] The authors should
explain about additivity of special sets [\dots] since it is not obvious
by default, feasibility condition does not explicitly implies
additivity. [\dots] the above condition ($\diamondsuit$)
$\sum_{i\in S}p_i\le D$ is crucial to relate your formulation to a 0/1
knapsack instance.
\end{comment}

\reply Thank you; the sentence was imprecise. We replaced it by an
explicit statement, \textbf{Fact~1} (Section~4.1), with a proof:
\emph{if all deadlines equal $D$, a set $S$ of sites can all be served on
time iff $\sum_{i\in S}p_i\le D$}, which is exactly your condition
($\diamondsuit$). The ``fixed set'' is an arbitrary subset $S$. The only
additivity used is that the \emph{last} site served from $S$ completes
at the sum of all of $S$'s occupancies, because the vehicle is never idle
and each $p_i$ does not depend on the predecessor (the defining property
of MWHED); this gives necessity. For sufficiency every completion time is
a partial sum of positive numbers and so is at most the total. The
proof of Theorem~2 now cites Fact~1, and the knapsack correspondence for
general weights is stated after the theorem.

\begin{comment}
2. [\dots] your claim on ``NP-complete of the 0/1 knapsack problem''
comes from polynomial reduction of PARTITION (one of the six popular
graph-based NP-complete problems [\dots]). This is a solidly smart
argument, if the above concern is clearly elucidated. Otherwise, a
graph-based pure proof for Theorem 2 possibly might give better insights
(ref. Delazeri and Ritt (2026)).
\end{comment}

\reply We removed the dependence on the knapsack citation: Theorem~2 now
gives a \emph{direct} polynomial reduction from \textsc{Partition} to
MWHED ($p_i=w_i=a_i$, $d_i=A/2$), so that the proof is self-contained
and also shows hardness when $w_i=p_i$; membership in NP is added. A
small remark on terminology: \textsc{Partition} is one of the six basic
NP-complete problems in Garey and Johnson but it is a number problem,
not a graph problem (the graph-based members of that list are Vertex
Cover, Clique and Hamiltonian Circuit). We did not give a graph-based
proof: MWHED has no graph structure once each visit is a round trip from
the depot, so there is no natural graph on which to base one.
\citet{delazeri2026} prove NP-completeness for a different, graph-based
model of wildfire suppression with simultaneous resources; Section~2
says so.

\begin{comment}
3. [\dots] the authors would elucidate more explicitly Theorem 5's
proof. In particular, would you remind readers the concepts (a)
transversal matroid, union-find structure, and (b) the related notation
$\alpha(n)$?
\end{comment}

\reply Section~4.4 now opens with a paragraph recalling the notions
used: matroid and its three axioms, the greedy algorithm, the
transversal matroid (sites against (vehicle, position) slots), and the
union--find structure with path compression, with $\alpha$ defined as
the inverse Ackermann function. The proof is rewritten in five steps:
(1) positions and slots; (2) a counting criterion for feasibility,
$N_S(k)\le mk$ for all $k$; (3) a direct proof that the feasible sets
satisfy the matroid augmentation property (so the transversal-matroid
fact is proved, not cited); (4) a short proof that the greedy algorithm
is optimal on any matroid; (5) the union--find implementation, with a
proof that it accepts a site exactly when the kept set plus that site is
feasible. Algorithm~3 is rewritten accordingly, and Example~5 now also
shows $m=2$ vehicles.

\begin{comment}
4. [\dots] do we agree that the problem of MWHED is obviously
data-driven, the proofs and optimal solutions obtained depend on both
accurate formulation and precise calibration of model parameters, upon
uncertainty? To some extent, would formulation of uncertainty [\dots]
(at not just variable level but also at parameter one) be considered in
next studies, where stochastic programming might be a potential
approach? Kindly, e.g. refer to problems of evacuation planning under
unsafe contexts and facility location under disruptive events (FLDE),
ref. [1, 2].
\end{comment}

\reply We agree. Section~6.3 (now ``Uncertain parameters'') was
rewritten to cover uncertainty in $p_i$, $d_i$ and $w_i$, not only in
the deadlines. It discusses (i) scenario-based and two-stage stochastic
formulations and which of our results survive (the earliest-deadline
lemma holds scenario by scenario but not simultaneously; a worked
example shows that sorting by expected deadline can be nearly twice as
bad as optimal), citing your two references \citep{wang2020evac,koca2023}
(we verified both: Computers \& Industrial Engineering 145, 106458, and
183, 109484; the database lists a single author for the second); (ii) a
robust-feasibility observation (feasibility is monotone in the
parameters, so a set that is on time for every realization in a box is a
feasible set of one MWHED instance, which also shows that full
robustness is too conservative in our case study); and (iii) calibration
and sensitivity analysis. In addition, the case study (Section~5.6) now
includes a Monte-Carlo sensitivity analysis over response speed, arrival
times and departure delay, which shows that the nominal-optimal plan is
brittle (fully on time in $20.7\%$ of $5{,}000$ draws) while the simpler
plan ``serve Paradise only'' is robust. Stochastic programming for
MWHED itself is listed as the main direction for future work; we have
not attempted a solution method for it here.

\section{Errors of our own corrected in this revision}\label{sec:own}

In the course of revising we re-checked the manuscript and the code and
found the following, which we correct and disclose.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Case study: deadline semantics.} The model's on-time test
counts the completion of the whole round trip (the ``return reading''),
but the narrative and the abstract speak of reaching the site before the
hazard (the ``arrival reading''). The submitted case study used the
return reading while describing arrival, and consequently claimed that
Concow and Magalia could not be reached at $50$~km/h although the crew
arrives after $31.5$ and $44.5$ minutes against fire-arrival minutes $52$
and $58$. Remark~1 now makes the two readings precise (the arrival
reading is an instance of MWHED with deadlines $d_i^{\mathrm{haz}}+p_i/2$)
and the case study uses it. The results change: at $80$~km/h the optimum
is Concow then Paradise ($26{,}928$), not Paradise alone. The return
reading is reported for comparison.
\item \textbf{Case study: baselines and wording.} Individually
infeasible sites are now deleted before every algorithm, including the
naive baseline (the submitted ``naive $=0$'' result used sites that
violate Assumption~1, so it did not illustrate Proposition~6). Statements
that the optimum ``saves'' a given number of people were replaced by the
on-time weight (population) of the sites served.
\item \textbf{Naive baseline.} Our naive EDD baseline kept dispatching to
sites that were already late, which made it a weak anchor; we added EDD
with skipping.
\item \textbf{Heeger--Hermelin.} The submitted text said that a small
number of distinct dispatch costs ``does not guarantee tractability''. The
result is $W[1]$-hardness in that parameter (no $f(k)\mathrm{poly}(n)$
algorithm), while for fixed $k$ the problem is polynomial; the text now
says this.
\item \textbf{Multiple vehicles.} The claim that the $m=1$ instance
``embeds'' by adding idle vehicles was wrong (extra vehicles can serve
sites); we now use blocker sites. The inference from
\citet{heegermolter2025} that the equal-cost case becomes hard with
several vehicles was also wrong, because that result requires release
dates, which MWHED does not have; Theorem~5 now shows tractability for
every $m$.
\item \textbf{Implementation.} The FPTAS guarantee relies on
Assumption~1 (so that $w_{\max}\le W^\ast$), which the code did not
enforce; both algorithms now delete infeasible sites first, and the code
was rewritten and checked against exhaustive search.
\item Minor: the dash convention in the dynamic-program example table was
described incorrectly; the explanation of why the FPTAS runtime was flat
in $\epsilon$ cited saturating weights, whereas the cause was $K=1$. Both
are fixed.
\end{enumerate}

We believe the revised manuscript is considerably stronger and more
honest about what is and is not new, and we thank the reviewers for the
comments that led to these changes.

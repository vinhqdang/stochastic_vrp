# Informative Pickup Routing with Shared Standby Vehicles

Consider $K$ operating vehicles, $B$ standby vehicles, and a dispatch time
$\tau$. All operating vehicles can make visits before $\tau$. Their visit
choices determine which pickup demands the dispatcher learns. At $\tau$, the
dispatcher assigns standby vehicles to unfinished routes. The central question
is how the shared standby capacity changes the value of learning pickup
demands early.

## Inputs and information

- Operating vehicle $k$ has capacity $Q_k$ and an assigned ordered customer
  list $R_k$. The lists are disjoint and cover all customers. The order of
  visits before $\tau$ can change. After $\tau$, the unserved customers on
  each route are served in the order inherited from $R_k$.
- Travel times, travel costs, and service durations are deterministic. Time
  is discretized into slots. A visit reveals the customer's pickup demand when
  service completes. All revealed demands are shared with the dispatcher.
- $\Omega$ is a finite set of complete pickup-demand scenarios, with
  probabilities $p_\omega$. Demand $d_i(\omega)$ at any customer is
  nonnegative and at most the capacity of every vehicle eligible to serve
  that customer. The joint law can encode dependence across routes.
- Standby vehicle $b$ has a capacity, a depot, and a fixed activation cost.
  It can take over at most one residual route. A residual route can use at
  most one standby vehicle.

At time $t$, let $s_t$ contain the locations, onboard loads, completed
customer sets, and in-progress actions and their completion times for all
operating vehicles. Let $\mathcal F_t^\pi$ be the history generated by the
actions and completed customer observations under policy $\pi$. Decisions
must be $\mathcal F_t^\pi$-measurable. In a scenario tree, two scenarios that
have produced the same observation and action history through $t$ must
receive the same decision at $t$. The observation history depends on the
policy because a skipped or unfinished visit exposes no demand.

## Feasible actions and objective

Before $\tau$, each idle vehicle may wait or start a visit to one of its
unserved assigned customers. A visit includes travel and service, and must
finish by $\tau$. The model uses a simple capacity rule for this phase. If
$q_k(t)$ is the current onboard load, a visit to $i$ may start only if
$q_k(t)+\max_{\omega:p_\omega>0} d_i(\omega)\le Q_k$. Completion updates
the load and removes $i$ from that route's unserved set. Pre-dispatch travel
cost on slot $t$ is $c_t(s_t,a_t)$. It can be charged at action start.

At $\tau$, $x_{kb}=1$ indicates that standby $b$ takes over residual route
$k$. For a known state $s_\tau$, let $E(s_\tau)$ be the set of eligible
standby–route pairs. This excludes finished routes and capacity-incompatible
vehicles. Feasible assignments satisfy

$$
x_{kb}\in\{0,1\},\qquad
x_{kb}=0\quad ((k,b)\notin E(s_\tau)),\qquad
\sum_b x_{kb}\le 1\quad(\forall k),\qquad
\sum_k x_{kb}\le 1\quad(\forall b).
$$

For each scenario, $C_{k0}(s_\tau,\omega)$ is the cost of the original
vehicle completing the residual route. $C_{kb}(s_\tau,\omega)$ is the
cost of activating standby $b$, returning primary $k$ to its depot, and
having $b$ complete that residual route from its own depot with an empty
load. Each mode includes travel and return to depot. In either mode, a
visit that would exceed capacity triggers a trip from that customer to
the serving vehicle's depot and back. The vehicle unloads at the depot
and then completes the pickup. Charge the extra travel plus the specified
emergency cost. This defines the continuation costs directly from the
scenario and ensures physical capacity feasibility. Continuations have
no shared constraints after dispatch.

The fleet problem is

$$
\min_{\pi,x}\;
\mathbb E \left[
    \sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1} c_t(s_t,a_t)
    +\sum_{k=1}^K C_{k0}(s_\tau,\omega)
    +\sum_{(k,b)\in E(s_\tau)}
          x_{kb}\bigl(C_{kb}(s_\tau,\omega)-C_{k0}(s_\tau,\omega)\bigr)
\right].
$$

Here $a_t=\pi_t(\mathcal F_t^\pi)$ is a feasible joint action, and $x$
is chosen from $\mathcal F_\tau^\pi$. Every customer is served exactly
once, either before dispatch or on its residual route. The model makes a
single pool-allocation decision and allows any feasible number of prior
visits. Customer time windows and repeated standby dispatch are outside
this formulation.

## Exact decomposition

Let $\mu_t$ denote the posterior scenario probabilities given the
information through $t$. At dispatch, define the edge value

$$
g_{kb}(s_\tau,\mu_\tau)
=\mathbb E_{\mu_\tau}[C_{k0}(s_\tau,\omega)-C_{kb}(s_\tau,\omega)].
$$

The optimal dispatch is a maximum-weight bipartite matching, with
standby vehicles allowed to remain idle. The terminal cost is

$$
V_\tau(s,\mu)
=\sum_k\mathbb E_\mu[C_{k0}(s,\omega)]
-\max_{x\in\mathcal X(s)}\sum_{(k,b)\in E(s)}g_{kb}(s,\mu)x_{kb}.
$$

For identical standby vehicles eligible for every unfinished route, the
matching reduces to taking the $B$ largest positive route values. The
pre-dispatch problem is a finite-horizon Bellman recursion. For a joint
action $a$, let $Y$ be the vector of demands exposed at the next slot, let
$T$ update the physical fleet state, and let $\mu^Y$ be the posterior after
conditioning on $Y$.

$$
V_t(s,\mu)
=\min_{a\in\mathcal A_t(s)}
\left\{c_t(s,a)+\mathbb E_{\mu}\bigl[V_{t+1}(T(s,a,Y),\mu^Y)\bigr]\right\}.
$$

The recursion is exact for the finite model. Its joint action and belief
spaces can grow exponentially, even though the terminal matching can be
solved in polynomial time.

## Standby scarcity makes information across independent routes complementary

Hold the physical dispatch state fixed and isolate the effect of information.
Suppose standby vehicles are identical and equally eligible. For any vector
of posterior expected net savings $g$, let $\Phi_B(g)$ be the sum of the
$B$ largest positive values. This section studies the dispatch objective
with local signals about net savings and no change in travel or eligibility.

**Single-route criterion.** Suppose revealing a signal on route $k$
changes only its score, from $g_k$ to a posterior score $G_k$ with
$\mathbb E[G_k]=g_k$. Let $\theta_k$ be the $B$-th highest positive
score among the other routes, or zero if fewer than $B$ others have
positive scores. The value of this signal is

$$
\mathbb E[(G_k-\theta_k)_+]-(g_k-\theta_k)_+.
$$

Indeed, for fixed scores on all other routes,
$\Phi_B(g)=\Phi_B(g_{-k})+(g_k-\theta_k)_+$. Thus a single signal has
strictly positive value exactly when the posterior score falls on both
sides of the allocation cutoff with positive probability.

**Proposition.** For every integer $B\ge 1$, there are $2B$ independent
routes and $B$ identical standby vehicles such that every individual
signal has zero value, yet revealing all signals increases expected net
savings by

$$
\frac{B}{2}\left(1-\frac{\binom{2B}{B}}{4^B}\right)
\ \ge\ \frac{B}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{B}{8}}.
$$

**Proof.** Assign one local signal to each route. On each of the first $B$
routes, its signal reveals a net saving of 4 or 8 with equal probability.
On each of the other $B$ routes, its signal reveals a net saving of 0 or 5
with equal probability. All signals are independent. With no observations,
dispatch to the first group because its mean savings are 6 rather than
2.5. Revealing a single first-group value leaves it above 2.5. Revealing a
single second-group value leaves it below 6. Thus every singleton signal
leaves the original allocation optimal and has zero expected value.

After all signals, replacing a first-group route valued at 4 with a
second-group route valued at 5 gains one unit. No other swap increases
value. Let $U$ count first-group routes valued at 4, and $V$ count
second-group routes valued at 5. Both are independent
$\operatorname{Binomial}(B,1/2)$. The incremental gain is
$\min(U,V)$. Since $U+(B-V)\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(2B,1/2)$,
the binomial identity
$\mathbb E|U-V|=B\binom{2B}{B}/4^B$ gives the exact expression.
Alternatively, $\mathbb E|U-V|\le\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(U-V)}
=\sqrt{B/2}$ gives the stated lower bound.

The proposition rules out any positive multiplicative guarantee for a
policy that buys only signals with positive immediate value. For example,
charge $1/16$ per signal. The policy buys none, whereas buying all signals
has positive net value for every $B\ge1$ and yields $\Theta(B)$ net value.
The proof concerns dispatch-level information value, with the service
state fixed. It separates the coupling created by a scarce standby fleet
from coupling created by correlated demands or by travel.

The two-visit pilot covers a single dispatch decision. Implementing the
fleet recursion and checking the proposition against prior work remain
open.

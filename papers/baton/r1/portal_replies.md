## Reply to Reviewer 1

We thank the reviewer for a careful and constructive report. All eight points have been addressed; the detailed response is in the attached letter. Where a new experiment did not come out in our favour, the manuscript reports it. In brief:

1. Stochastic assumptions and state sufficiency. Assumption 1 now requires only that the running load be a Markov chain with stochastically monotone kernels. This covers independent increments and a Gaussian day-factor model, in which the load is sufficient for the factor, and it makes (k, W_k) a sufficient state for the handoff problem. For the three-action problem the recursion is exact under independence and misspecified under factor dependence, which the state-conditional variant BATON-cf corrects; the manuscript now says so. A new experiment (Section 4.5, Table 7) varies the demand correlation (rho = 0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9) and adds a route-level day factor. A BATON variant keeps the highest saving of the implementable policies under every law. BATON itself is tied with its strongest competitor in three of the ten law-gate rows and loses one (rho = 0.9, Det plans, to an equal-data three-action dynamic program, by 1.1 points), which BATON-cf reverses. Under independence an exact dynamic program (Table 8) shows BATON at 94% of the exact optimum on Det plans (59% on the slack SAA plans). A direct test on 50,000 paths per route finds no significant violations of monotonicity (significant violations in 0.2%, 0.1% and 0.0% of bin pairs at rho = 0, 0.6, 0.9), though the test has limited power: an injected dip of 10% (25%) of the mean continuation cost is detected in only 64% (89%) of cases, so the evidence is consistent with, but does not prove, monotonicity.

2. Shared standby capacity. We now state that the default model is pay-per-use from a pool shared across the operation, and report the pool size the policy would need. A new experiment (Section 4.6, Table 10) reserves a pool per plan or per metropolitan group of ten plans, with first-come-first-served dispatch, a decision at the refusal stop for refused routes, a shadow price chosen on the training days, and a sweep of holding costs. A reserved vehicle serves at most one route a day, so at the standby day rate reservation never beats pay-per-use; at a holding cost of 5 it does on 40/40 packed Det plans and on 4/4 metropolitan pools. Given a cap, shadow pricing beats ignoring it wherever the cap binds (up to 9.4% on the metropolitan pools).

3. Depot-return transition. Section 3.5 now describes the post-return load, the residual capacity, repeated returns and the extra Bellman term. The reset-to-zero convention is conservative: the exact post-return state is minus the delivered volume. Pricing the exact reset raises the saving (for example from 52.2% to 57.9% on Salhi-Nagy), and the manuscript now recommends it for deployment.

4. Threshold-policy comparison. We added a position-dependent threshold (one tuned level per stop, which can represent the optimal boundary; its search starts from the tuned global threshold and from BATON-ho's boundaries), a cost-scaled rollout, a two-lever rule that gives a tuned threshold the depot return as well, and an equal-data three-action dynamic program. For the handoff decision the position-dependent threshold comes within 0.1-3.3 points of BATON-ho on every gate, as the reviewer expected. The two-lever rule stays 1.4-10.3 points below BATON, so the gain comes from pricing the levers. On the five conservative gates BATON leads the strongest of all competitors by 6.5-10.3 points; on Det plans the equal-data three-action program ties it (Tables 2, 3, 5).

5. Computational fairness. All data-driven policies use the same 1,000 training days; only the two high-data reference programs use more. The protocol is now documented (no separate validation set, which favours the tuned competitors; fixed seeds). A data-budget experiment from 100 to 20,000 days (Section 4.7) shows BATON ahead of the tuned threshold at every budget. Table A.5 separates offline fitting (24 ms per route for BATON) from online decisions (about 1.2 µs each). The RL baseline, retrained on the same CPU, needs 1-3 minutes.

6. Statistical analysis. The experimental unit is now the instance within a gate (n = 40; routes of a plan share their days). Table 3 gives paired mean differences with 95% bootstrap intervals, win counts, rank-biserial effect sizes and Holm-adjusted tests: against the strongest competitor, +6.5 to +10.3 points on the conservative gates, all intervals above zero, and a tie on the deterministic gate (+0.0, [-0.4, +0.4]). Because the strongest competitor is chosen on the same instances, its interval re-selects the competitor in every bootstrap resample and its p-value is an intersection-union test over all candidates. The robustness tables now carry paired intervals for their key contrast, and the RL comparison is tested at the instance level. Table 4 adds CVaR95 of the daily plan bill and the daily emergency probability. Route-level results are released as supplementary data.

7. Methodological positioning. The introduction now separates the model, the structural results (Propositions 1-4) and the method, which is explicitly an adaptation of regression-based stopping, with novelty claimed only for the isotonic step, the valuation of the post-return state and the menu selection. We added the classical antecedents (Serfozo 1976; Puterman 1994; Müller and Stoyan 2002; Yang et al. 2000; Minis and Tatarakis 2011; Tatarakis and Minis 2009) and corrected the convergence citation (Egloff 2005; Zanger 2013 instead of Clément et al., whose analysis covers a fixed linear basis). All four propositions are machine-checked in Lean 4 (Mathlib), and the manuscript lists the modelling abstractions of the formalisation.

8. Scope of the conclusions. A new paragraph "Scope of the conclusions" (Section 5) states the limits: no time windows, uniform lateness penalties, fixed routes and no integrated planning, shared resources only through the standby pool, no standby response time, and calibrated rather than operational data. The managerial findings are qualified accordingly; in particular we withdrew the claim that urban geography makes the depot return worthless, since our instances cannot separate it from plan slack and demand profile.

## Reply to Reviewer 2

We thank the reviewer for a thorough and helpful report. All major and minor comments have been addressed; details are in the attached letter.

Major 1 (independence vs. rho = 0.6). The monotonicity result (now Proposition 4) needs only a stochastically monotone Markov load (revised Assumption 1), not independence; this covers a Gaussian day-factor model. For the benchmark copula it holds approximately, and we now say so. We ran the suggested sweep rho = 0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9 plus a day factor (Table 7). Monotonicity violations are at the false-positive level (0.2%, 0.1%, 0.1%, 0.0%; 0.6% under the day factor), but the test has limited power (an injected 10% dip is detected in only 64% of cases), so we present the isotonic restriction as a reasonable approximation outside the theorem, not as proven. A BATON variant stays best among implementable policies under every law; BATON itself ties in three rows and loses one (rho = 0.9 on Det plans) to an equal-data three-action program, a loss that BATON-cf reverses. Under independence BATON attains 94% of the exact optimum on Det plans and 59% on SAA plans (Table 8). All implementable policies save more as rho grows.

Major 2 (fresh-start bias). We identify and quantify three biases against a high-data reference value from 50,000 independent paths (Table A.3): (i) suboptimal downstream policy (upward, as you note), (ii) in-sample optimism (downward), and (iii) under dependence, the post-return suffix is correlated with the state that triggered the return (downward on exactly those days). On Salhi-Nagy, (i) and (ii) are small and nearly cancel (error +0.50% of the handoff price in sample, +0.09% out of sample; the saving moves from 52.2% to 51.9% with the reference value). (iii) matters on city routes, and a state-conditional fresh-start value (BATON-cf) removes it (city: 5.6% to 9.4% without deployment selection). BATON-cf, introduced during the revision, is reported beside BATON in the main tables.

Major 3 (action set, third lever). The conclusion now states the boundaries of the action set: actions that end the route at a known price, or reset the load to a known level. A partial handoff is outside it, and we describe what it would require. We now state plainly that the depot return pays where plans carry slack and depots are central (Salhi-Nagy: 53.0% vs 42.3% handoff-only), and rarely on our city instances (10.6% vs 10.6%). We had attributed the latter to urban geography; re-planning the city instances with slack (SAA gate) all but removed breaches (reactive cost 0.04 per plan-day), so geography cannot be separated from plan slack and demand profile, and the manuscript now says exactly that.

Major 4 (quantifying Proposition 2). A new Proposition 3 (machine-checked in Lean 4) shows that the myopic stopping time never exceeds the optimal one and bounds its excess cost by the cost of interventions on days that would have completed cleanly, plus forgone price declines. The claims now concern the myopic break-even threshold, not every fixed threshold. Table 6 evaluates it: regret 3.8% of reactive cost against a bound of 7.7%, smallest on the city plans (2.0%) and short routes. A threshold tuned on realised cost is not covered by the bound; its excess over BATON-ho is 1.0% of reactive cost on tight Det plans, 0.4% on city routes and 3.0% on SAA plans. So a threshold is good enough on tight plans for the handoff decision; the large gaps on conservative gates come mainly from the depot return.

Major 5 (co-optimization). Yes, we consider it the most important direction, because it targets the first-stop risk that no execution policy can reach. It was not implemented because it would remove the common-plan design that isolates the execution contribution, and because it requires the fitted execution cost inside the planner's inner loop. The conclusion now says this.

Major 6 (exchangeability). New experiment with promotion days (Table 9), all strategies fitted on the same 1,000-day history. On promotion days BATON saves 29.7% with day-type-specific fits (about 197 promotion days), 29.4% with a pooled fit and 28.3% with a stale fit; over all days the split fits are no better than pooling (27.6% vs 27.6%), because each sees fewer days. The practical rule: keep known day types in one history; fit separately only with long histories per type; re-fit on a rolling window where the mix drifts.

Minor 1 (RL). Section 4.4 now discusses DQN, PPO and attention/graph architectures and where they could help. The RL baseline was retrained on a consistent route bundle, on the same CPU, and is compared at the instance level; we state that our runs do not rule out that longer training or other architectures would narrow the gap.

Minor 2 (clairvoyant bound). The explanation now appears in the abstract, introduction, Section 3.6, Section 4.2 and the conclusion; tables label the oracle "handoff-only", and we added the three-action clairvoyant bound, of which BATON attains 52%-68% on the Dethloff gates.

Minor 3 (zero pickups). Added deliver-only twins with 25% and 50% zero-pickup customers, re-planned (Table 5). Savings shrink because breaches become rarer (BATON 4.9% and 3.1%); at 50% BATON is tied with the equal-data dynamic program and reaches only 47% of the high-data reference, which we report.

Minor 4 (Figure 2). Split into two figures (Figures 2 and 3) with print-size fonts.

## Reply to Reviewer 3

We thank the reviewer for the careful reading. All five points have been addressed; details are in the attached letter.

1. Standby vs. emergency vehicles. Standby vehicles are reserved capacity under a pre-agreed rate, dispatched before a breach, and they keep customers on schedule. Emergency vehicles are hired on the spot market after a breach, at surge prices, with all downstream customers late. A new paragraph in Section 3.1 explains this. In the default model the standby pool is shared and billed per use, and we now report the pool the policy would need (for BATON a median of 3.5 and a maximum of 7 vehicles per Det plan). A new experiment with a reserved pool (Section 4.6) shows that holding standby vehicles in advance pays only when they cost well under the standby day rate and handoffs are frequent.

2. Holding cost, Assumption 2 and Eq. (7). The standby day rate in the handoff price is the holding cost, charged per vehicle-day used; the reserved-pool experiment models holding costs paid in advance and reports the break-even holding cost. Assumption 2 now requires only a non-increasing emergency price: no ordering between handoff and emergency prices is needed anywhere, and Eq. (7) is presented as a property of our calibration. A new configuration with the standby rate above the emergency price (Table 11) confirms that the policy simply stops using the dominated handoff: BATON saves 36.8% there vs 1.4% for the tuned threshold.

3. "Lowest cost" vs. oracle. Thank you; the claim now reads "lowest cost of every implementable policy" throughout. The oracle is clairvoyant (it knows the day's demands) and restricted to the handoff lever; it is a bound, not a competitor, and the tables now separate reference points from competitors. It stays ahead of BATON on the Det and W-DRO gates, where the depot return is worth little; on the other four gates BATON exceeds it because a depot return can prevent a breach at a price no handoff can match. We also added the clairvoyant bound for the full three-action menu, which lies above every policy.

4. BATON in Tables 3 and 5 (now Tables A.1 and A.2). Both now report BATON. In the synthetic scenarios the depot return is priced flat at half a handoff (collect-then-deliver: 69.1%). In the RL comparison, BATON-ho is the like-for-like policy (32.9%) and full BATON saves 33.7%.

5. City: BATON below DP50k and the oracle. Both are reference points rather than competitors. The oracle knows the future, so its lead cannot be attained. DP50k uses 50 times more data; with the depot return idle on city routes, the gap (10.6% vs 12.1%) is to a reference with more data and a different estimator, so it is not attributed to data alone; the data-budget experiment (Section 4.7) indicates that data explain most of it, since BATON reaches 12.9% with 20,000 training days, slightly above DP50k.
